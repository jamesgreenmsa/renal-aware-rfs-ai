Section 2

2The Clinical Problem

Refeeding syndrome (RFS) is a potentially serious electrolyte disturbance, characterized by acute hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia, that can follow initial caloric exposure after a period of restriction. The four problems below define why the existing surveillance infrastructure systematically misses it, particularly in patients with concurrent acute kidney injury.

🍴

When does the caloric clock start?

Standard RFS surveillance anchors electrolyte monitoring to formal nutrition orders: enteral or parenteral nutrition initiation. But many ICU patients receive material quantities of intravenous dextrose, dextrose-containing fluids, or propofol (which carries 1.1 kcal/mL as a 10% lipid emulsion) well before a formal nutrition order is placed. If the metabolic clock starts with IV dextrose exposure, the conventional anchor systematically lags behind the actual physiological event.

⏱

The timing gap

In preliminary MIMIC-IV work, the median interval from last known-normal electrolyte to first observed low was 12.9 hours. Among patients who developed a qualifying deficiency before formal nutrition began, the median interval from that first low to EN/PN initiation was 35.8 hours. The conventional monitoring window, anchored to the nutrition order, would have been open for zero of those 35.8 hours.

🬸

Kidney function changes the picture

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is common in the ICU and directly alters electrolyte handling. Phosphate, potassium, and magnesium are renally excreted; AKI impairs clearance and can simultaneously cause retention and abnormal distribution. Electrolyte deficiencies in a patient with concurrent AKI may reflect nutritional depletion, impaired renal handling, or both. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and distinguishing them requires integrating renal function trajectories, not just electrolyte values.

📋

Documentation gaps compound the problem

AKI is substantially under-documented in structured clinical records. In a separate surgical ICU cohort, approximately 75.7% of AKI-coded encounters meeting strict creatinine-based criteria had no mention of AKI in the discharge summary. This documents a discordance between laboratory trajectories and narrative records; it does not establish that treating clinicians failed to recognize AKI at the bedside. A surveillance system that relies on coded diagnoses or discharge summaries to identify renal context may therefore be missing a critical input for electrolyte interpretation.

The remaining challenge

Detecting an early electrolyte abnormality is only the first step. Determining whether it reflects nutritional depletion, renal dysfunction, or overlapping processes requires integrating evidence that conventional surveillance does not routinely bring together.

The sections below walk through what a renal-aware framework would look like in practice, how it distinguishes these patterns on synthetic patient cases, and what this pilot proposes to build and validate.

A 58-year-old with septic shock is admitted to the surgical ICU. She has not eaten meaningfully in four days. Section 4  ·  Proposed method

4AI Design: Two Uses, One Decisive Question

The pilot uses AI in two complementary ways, and then asks a single question that determines which approach is worth pursuing further in a larger study.

Use 1 — Predict early

A prediction model fires at the moment a patient begins IV dextrose infusion, before any formal nutrition orders are written. It estimates the likelihood of a qualifying electrolyte deficiency in the upcoming window, so the team can consider whether closer monitoring is warranted.

Aligns with Aim 1. Requires only structured EHR features available at the caloric-exposure landmark.

Use 2 — Interpret afterward

After an electrolyte deficiency is observed, a convergence framework integrates nutritional timing, electrolyte trajectory, renal context, and prediction outputs to assemble an auditable evidence summary: what was happening, what explains it, and how certain we can be.

Aligns with Aim 3. Outputs are for retrospective review and research adjudication, not direct clinical use.

The pilot would develop a modular, auditable evidence integration framework. The architecture is conceptual: no functioning system exists. An interpretation layer could use rules, machine learning, or a combination, subject to evaluation against an independent clinical reference standard.

🍴

Nutrition module

IV dextrose exposure, timing, and quantity. EN/PN order type, timing, and rate. Caloric anchor assignment.

📉

Electrolyte module

Serial phosphate, potassium, magnesium. Trajectory features: rate of decline, nadir, recovery pattern, lab frequency.

🬸

Renal context module

Serial creatinine, AKI stage, CKD history, renal replacement therapy. Longitudinal trajectory, not point-in-time values.

↓

AI-assisted evidence integration layer

Temporal alignment Aligns evidence streams on a shared time axis anchored to clinical events.
Signal concordance Identifies where nutrition, electrolyte, and renal evidence agree or conflict.
Competing explanations Represents multiple plausible attributions; does not force a single classification.
Uncertainty flagging Flags cases where evidence is insufficient for confident attribution.
Source provenance Audit trail linking each output to the structured data points that support it.
Interpretability Clinician-readable summary; no black-box scores without supporting evidence.
↓

Output: patient-level evidence summary

For each reviewed case: an auditable, time-aligned summary of nutritional exposure, electrolyte trajectory, and renal context; a classification of evidence concordance (concordant / conflicting / indeterminate); candidate explanations with supporting evidence; and an uncertainty flag where applicable. Designed for comparison against independent clinical adjudication, not for direct clinical use.

The decisive comparison

The pilot will evaluate three approaches in sequence. The comparison is structured so that the simplest approach that performs adequately is the right answer, not the most complex one. If the learned model does not outperform the renal-aware rules, the rules are the better result. A simpler, more explainable system that performs equally well is preferable to a complex one that offers no benefit.

A

Conventional assessment

Standard nutrition and laboratory evaluation without renal context or caloric-anchor adjustment. The current clinical baseline.

B

Renal-aware rules

Explicit if-then logic incorporating renal context, AKI stage, and caloric-anchor timing. Fully auditable; requires no training data or labeled cases.

C

Learned ML model

Model trained on independently adjudicated clinical phenotype labels. Can learn trajectory patterns the rules may miss. Conditional on sufficient adjudicated training data with a held-out evaluation set.

The core question

Does approach (C) outperform approach (B) against independent clinical adjudication? If yes, the complexity of learned integration is justified. If no, the transparent rules are the better tool for this problem, and the pilot has established that clearly. Either outcome advances the science.

Approach (C) requires retrospectively adjudicated case labels with a separate held-out evaluation set. The choice of model class remains an open design question subject to sample size, label quality, and AI Methods collaborator input.

6Pilot Deliverables and Open Decisions

The LOI maps to three coordinated aims. The primary vehicle for proof-of-concept is MIMIC-IV; the institutional replication target is the Rutgers Clinical Research Data Warehouse (CRDW), with feasibility assessment as an explicit Aim 1 deliverable. The pilot does not promise clinical deployment.

Three coordinated aims

1

Incident electrolyte deficiency prediction model

Develop and internally validate a prediction model using structured EHR features available at prespecified clinical landmarks. The primary landmark is first qualifying caloric exposure, including IV dextrose; a secondary landmark at formal nutrition-order initiation includes only patients still free of the outcome at that point. Building on MIMIC-IV feature engineering (6,843 admissions, 54.2% RFS prevalence). CRDW data completeness and timestamp fidelity for administered dextrose, EN/PN, serial electrolytes, and renal measures assessed first as an explicit feasibility deliverable; modeling proceeds if data support it.

Deliverable: Validated prediction model (MIMIC-IV) + CRDW feasibility assessment
2

CRDW electrolyte-timing characterization and renal-context assessment

Characterize the timing and predictors of first observed electrolyte deficiency relative to initial caloric exposure and formal nutrition initiation in the Rutgers CRDW, conditional on data availability from Aim 1. Assess whether the MIMIC-IV finding of 38.4% pre-EN/PN deficiency generalizes to the Rutgers cohort using aligned definitions. Report laboratory observation intervals and assess associations with kidney function, AKI, CKD, and renal replacement therapy, without inferring causation. Includes assessment of AKI discharge-summary documentation concordance as a secondary characterization supporting the Aim 3 prototype.

Deliverable: CRDW timing characterization report + transportability assessment for Aim 3
3

Renal-aware evidence convergence prototype

Integrate nutritional exposure timing, serial electrolyte trajectories, Aim 1 prediction outputs, and longitudinal renal context into an auditable patient-level interpretation framework. Compare integrated framework against conventional nutrition and laboratory-based assessment using retrospectively reviewed MIMIC-IV cases. Evaluate agreement, uncertainty recognition, and interpretability against independent clinical adjudication. Deliverable is an offline proof of concept, not a clinical tool.

Deliverable: Retrospectively evaluated offline prototype + R21-ready plan
